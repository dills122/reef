# Calcify capacity profile: how other systems handle lagged state commitments (research, 2026-10-14)

Status: research note, not a decision. Prepared at the owner's request ("see what the experts do, how big projects handle this") to steer the three open questions of design section 5.6 (Q11 batch size K, Q12 undo ring cost, Q13 the trie thread ceiling) in [CALCIFY_FINANCE_STATE_COMMITMENT_DESIGN.md](CALCIFY_FINANCE_STATE_COMMITMENT_DESIGN.md).
Nothing here was run. Claims are from the sources listed at the end; where I only have a search summary, or only my own background knowledge, the text says so. Most evidence comes from blockchain execution engines, because they face the same problem (a state commitment on the critical path of a high-rate deterministic state machine) and publish their reasoning; they differ from Calcify in that they also need consensus, light-client proofs and adversarial participants, which Calcify does not need today (design 3.5, 4.3).

## 2. What this says about our questions

**It validates the shape.** Lagging the state commitment so it leaves the critical path is mainstream, for the same reason as ours: CometBFT lags by one block, Ethereum's draft EIP-7862 proposes one, Monad uses three. Monad's design is the closest relative of section 5.6: ordering/decisions proceed, the root of an older state rides in later blocks as a divergence detector, and a node that disagrees **rolls back to a retained earlier state and re-executes**, which is our undo-ring-plus-window-replay restore. Nothing in the sources suggests a better rule than "retain a bounded window of pre-state, restore by replaying it".

**Q11, the size of K and the exposure window.** Others lag by 1 to 3 *blocks*, and a block is a batch of many transactions at hundreds of milliseconds to a second. Our K = 100 decisions is about 5 ms at 20,000 decisions/s, a far smaller window than any of them, and we measured no throughput loss at K = 1,000 (about 50 ms). Practice would support a window of the order of 100 ms to 1 s, which at our rate is K of roughly 2,000 to 20,000. Two things are not answered by the sources: the ring grows with K (entries of `2K - 1` decisions of before-images, megabytes at these sizes), and the replay at restore is `2K - 1` decisions (about 25 microseconds each in the prototype, so 100 ms at K = 2,000 and under a second at K = 20,000). **Suggestion:** define the window in time, not decisions, derive K from the sustained rate, default to about 100 ms, and measure K = 2,000 and 5,000 before fixing it.

**Q12, the cost of the undo ring.** Practice keeps a **bounded window of reverse diffs or pre-state** (Geth state history, EIP-7862's pre-state retention, Monad's rollback target, TigerBeetle's checkpoint ring). The one hot-path detail visible in the sources is that Geth writes its reverse diffs **in batches when layers merge to disk**, not per transaction. That suggests three options for us, in the order I would look at them:
1. **Derive before-images from the store's own changelog (no extra put).** Every entity put is already a changelog record, so the previous value of a key sits in the changelog before the window's records, provided the changelog is not compacted past the window. Kafka's `min.compaction.lag.ms` is a minimum time a record stays uncompacted (default 0); the Apache docs promise it will not be cleaned before that lag, not when it will be. The changelog topic is provisioned by the registrar from the binding (design 6.1), so its configuration is under our control. Restore already reads the changelog. Unverified: whether Streams' restore and the evidence reader tolerate it, and what a lag of `2K` decisions costs in disk (small).
2. **Write the ring in batches**, one put per batch of decisions rather than per decision. It must be in the same transaction as the entity writes it describes, or a crash between them leaves a ring that does not cover the table; that needs checking against how Streams commits.
3. **The per-decision ring put as written in 5.6** (about 12 percent more puts per input by S3d's count of 8).
The throughput cost of (3) is unmeasured; (1) should be designed first because it removes the cost.

**Q13, the trie thread ceiling (about 34k decisions/s above 5 million entities).** Two practices apply:
- **Parallelize inside the commitment.** Reth hashes dirty subtries in parallel above a threshold and keeps a warm in-memory trie across blocks; QMDB pipelines Merkleization and keeps upper nodes in memory. For a hash-keyed trie like ours, splitting the key space on the first byte or two into independent subtries, each updated by its own thread, then combining their roots, is the obvious analogue. It changes the root definition (and the goldens), so it is a design change, but it keeps proofs.
- **Drop the tree.** Solana replaced its epoch accounts hash with a per-block lattice hash: O(touched) adds and subtracts, no sorting, no tree, parallel by construction, no proofs. Our design needs no inclusion proofs today (4.3) and rejected the multiset accumulator only because its 3,072-bit bound had zero margin and its spec was incomplete (4.2). LtHash is the successor that addresses the margin (2,048 bytes, 128-bit target) but has a public cryptanalysis thread to read before relying on it, and needs BLAKE3 XOF, which the JDK lacks (a SHA-256 expansion would need 64 hashes per entity and is likely slower than the trie). Worth a spike, not a decision.

**Things the sources do not give us.** Nobody in the sources rotates the ledger; blockchains carry one state forever and use epochs or snapshots, so rotation (Decision 1) has no precedent here beyond Solana's epoch hashes. None of the numbers above are comparable with ours: different hardware, signature and consensus work, and vendor-authored benchmarks (QMDB, Reth) that I did not see independently reproduced.

## 3. Verification practice worth borrowing

TigerBeetle tests a deterministic state machine with a whole-cluster simulator that injects disk, clock and network faults. Section 9.15's cuts and failure injection are a hand-made version; a seeded simulator that crashes the processor at random points and checks restore-then-continue equals never-stopped would cover the lagged restore much more cheaply than enumerating cuts. LMAX's rule that state is entirely derivable from the input journal is the same property our window replay relies on.

## 4. Suggested next steps (for the owner to accept or change)

1. Re-state the exposure window in time (default about 100 ms) and measure K = 2,000 and 5,000 in the lane. No owner decision needed first.
2. Design option (1) for Q12 (before-images from the changelog) and check it against Streams restore; only measure the per-decision ring if (1) fails.
3. Two spikes for Q13, in this order: key-space-sharded trie on several threads; then LtHash with a real BLAKE3 library. Read the 2026 LtHash cryptanalysis before the second.
4. Add a seeded crash simulator to the 9.15 plan.
5. Run the end-to-end measurement (lane, projector and Postgres on one machine) before spending more on the commitment (section 5).
6. Do not take any claim in section 1 as a Calcify capacity claim.

## 5. Addendum (2026-10-14): the projector and SQL side

The first pass covered the commitment only. The projector (CAL-FIN-P1: about 12.5k decisions/s with the product's keys, 17k to 21k without) is probably the binding term, so this pass asks how others get an ordered ledger into a SQL read model at high rates. Sources: search summaries unless marked fetched.

- **The read model is a downstream consumer of the log, not part of the write path.** TigerBeetle's CDC is a separate, single-instance process that resumes from the last event acknowledged by the broker, is at-least-once, and expects consumers to be idempotent (keyed on transfer ID); duplicates are possible. The event-sourcing guidance for projections says the same: keep the checkpoint in the **same database and the same transaction** as the read-model rows, make every apply idempotent, skip positions at or below the checkpoint on replay, and design the projection so it can be rebuilt from zero. Calcify's projector already does all of these (checkpoint row, sequence-keyed idempotence, one transaction per batch, rebuild by replay); the research confirms the design and adds nothing to change.
- **Batching.** A Tiger Data benchmark (vendor, fetched summary) finds `INSERT ... UNNEST` and prepared multi-row statements beat text `COPY` for batches of about 10,000 rows or fewer, while binary `COPY` wins at large batches (around 100,000) because it bypasses server-side parsing. Our measured batches are 120 to 5,000 decisions, which is the range where `unnest` is the right tool, and our own spike agrees (unnest ahead of text COPY at 120 and 1,000 per call; COPY caught up at 5,000). Binary COPY is only worth testing if batches grow past tens of thousands.
- **Foreign keys on a hot write path.** The PostgreSQL documentation recommends dropping foreign keys for large loads because a bulk check is cheaper than per-row checks. Every referencing insert runs an index lookup and takes `FOR KEY SHARE` on the parent row; deferring the constraint helps per-row checking, not the lock, and the pending-trigger queue still grows. A PostgreSQL commit (2026, per a mailing-list summary, not verified in a release) adds a fast path for foreign-key checks reporting about 1.8x on bulk inserts with an int key, which hints the cost is real and mainstream. Practitioner advice for live tables is to keep keys but make parents rarely updated, or to drop keys on the hot table and validate by other means. **For Calcify this supports the CAL-FIN-P1 finding** (the four foreign keys to the checkpoint cost about 40 percent) and the proposal to drop them: the checkpoint row is already locked `FOR UPDATE` in the same transaction, so the key adds a second, weaker lock and a lookup per row for no extra guarantee. The attempt-to-obligation key and the action unique are different (integrity guards the transaction does not otherwise enforce) and cost 5 to 7 percent each; keep them unless a replacement argument is written.
- **Durability knobs.** The sources agree that `synchronous_commit = off` is the largest single throughput lever and that it can lose recently acknowledged commits; `full_page_writes` should stay on on ordinary filesystems; `wal_compression` and longer checkpoints reduce WAL volume. **CAL-FIN-P1 found none of these mattered on the test machine** (fsync and synchronous commit off changed nothing), so for a financial projection, where the log is the source of truth and the projection is rebuildable, the durable settings can stay at their defaults. The sources do not cover a fast NVMe or a managed Postgres, where the answer may differ.
- **Sources that did not exist.** I found no published benchmark of a ledger projector at 20,000 rows per second with foreign keys on; the vendor benchmarks are single-table insert tests.

**Net effect on the plan:** the research does not change the projector's design (idempotent, checkpoint in the same transaction, set-based batches) and supports removing the redundant checkpoint foreign keys; it does not tell us whether the projector holds 20k decisions/s alongside the lane on one machine, which only the end-to-end run can.

Additional sources: [TigerBeetle CDC](https://docs.tigerbeetle.com/operating/cdc/); [Tiger Data, benchmarking PostgreSQL batch ingest](https://www.tigerdata.com/blog/benchmarking-postgresql-batch-ingest); [Tiger Data, the hidden cost of Postgres constraints at scale](https://www.tigerdata.com/blog/hidden-cost-postgres-constraints-scale) (vendor); [PostgreSQL, populating a database](https://postgresql.org/docs/18/populate.html); [PostgreSQL commit, fast path for foreign key checks](https://www.postgresql.org/message-id/E1w7R6y-002Fk8-1I@gemulon.postgresql.org); [Confluent JDBC sink connector](https://docs.confluent.io/kafka-connectors/jdbc/current/sink-connector/overview.html) (at-least-once plus idempotent upsert, `batch.size`); event-sourcing projection guidance (several practitioner pages and a vendor extension, low weight).
